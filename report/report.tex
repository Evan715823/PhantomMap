%%%%%%%%% ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
   Vision--language models such as Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct
   \cite{qwen25vl} and LLaVA-NeXT-Mistral-7B \cite{llavanext}
   hallucinate objects that are not present in the input image. Prior
   work diagnoses this either by probing internal attention
   \cite{eazy,opera,halloc} or by querying an external object detector
   \cite{woodpecker}. We take a complementary and previously-unexplored
   angle: the \emph{first-party readout}. When a VLM hallucinates, we
   simply ask it -- through its public prompt interface -- to draw a
   bounding box around the phantom object, and then analyse the
   resulting boxes at scale. Across 9{,}000 (image, object) probes on
   the three POPE \cite{pope} splits, we collect $2{,}806$ phantom
   bounding boxes from the two models and show that phantom boxes
   consistently lie $18\%$--$32\%$ further from the image centre and
   cover $36\%$--$55\%$ less area than boxes drawn for real objects.
   A simple shape-and-logit feature set over the phantom box, trained
   with logistic regression, yields a \emph{training-free}
   hallucination detector that reaches $\mathrm{AUROC}=0.916$ on
   Qwen2.5-VL and $0.873$ on LLaVA-NeXT, both exceeding the
   internal-feature HALP baseline of $0.787$ \cite{halp} despite
   requiring nothing beyond the VLM's API. We release all code and
   raw phantom boxes. Where a hallucination is drawn turns out to be
   at least as informative as what it names.
\end{abstract}

%%%%%%%%% INTRO
\section{Introduction}

Object hallucination is the single most-cited failure mode of modern
multimodal systems \cite{hallsurvey,pope}. Two traditions have
emerged to study it. \emph{Internal probes} such as EAZY
\cite{eazy}, OPERA \cite{opera}, and HalLoc \cite{halloc} open up the
model and analyse image-token attention or output text-token
confidence. \emph{External verifiers} such as Woodpecker
\cite{woodpecker} send the VLM's caption to a separate object
detector and flag anything the detector cannot place.

Both views are powerful, but neither matches what an end user
actually sees. An end user only sees the VLM's public output. If
that output asserts an object exists, a natural follow-up is
\emph{``where, specifically, is it?''} Modern grounded VLMs
\cite{qwen25vl} will cheerfully answer with a bounding box. When the
object does not exist in the image, the bounding box is a
\emph{phantom}.

Phantoms are interesting in two ways. First, as a \emph{descriptive}
object: does a VLM place its phantoms anywhere in pixel space, or is
there a systematic structure -- a centre bias, a size bias, a
model-specific signature? Second, as a \emph{diagnostic} signal: can
the geometry of the phantom box, combined with the logit confidence
the model assigns to the emitted coordinates, be used as a
training-free hallucination detector?

This paper answers both. Our contributions are:
\textbf{(i)} a first-party spatial atlas (Fig.~\ref{fig:atlas}) of
$2{,}806$ phantom and $4{,}971$ honest boxes collected from
Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct and LLaVA-NeXT-Mistral-7B on the three POPE
splits; we show that phantom boxes concentrate \emph{further} from
the image centre and are \emph{smaller} than honest boxes,
consistently across both models;
\textbf{(ii)} a training-free logistic-regression detector over
eight shape/location/logit features of the phantom box, reaching
$\mathrm{AUROC} = 0.916$ (Qwen) and $0.873$ (LLaVA), both above the
internal-feature HALP baseline of $0.787$ \cite{halp}. Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}
gives the flavour of the finding at a glance.

%%%%%%%%% RELATED WORK
\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Object hallucination benchmarks.} POPE \cite{pope}
formalised object hallucination as a yes/no existence probe on COCO
\cite{mscoco}, with three negative-mining splits (random, popular,
adversarial). We use all three in this work. HallusionBench and
AMBER \cite{amber} extend the task to multi-dimensional attribute
queries. None of these ask a VLM \emph{where} a hallucinated object
is.

\paragraph{Internal-attention probes.} EAZY \cite{eazy} traces
hallucinated object tokens back to $\sim\!3$ high-attention image
tokens -- the \emph{Hallucinatory Image Tokens} -- and shows that
zeroing them out suppresses the hallucination. OPERA \cite{opera}
identifies an ``over-trust'' attention sink and penalises it at
decode time. HalLoc \cite{halloc} localises hallucinations at the
\emph{output text-token} level. All three require model internals.

\paragraph{External-detector verification.} Woodpecker
\cite{woodpecker} chains a VLM with Grounding DINO; any object the
detector cannot confirm is flagged. This trades one oracle for
another and assumes a sufficiently general detector.

\paragraph{Feature-based detection.} HALP \cite{halp} trains a
hallucination classifier on the VLM's internal visual features and
reaches $\mathrm{AUROC}=0.787$ on Qwen2.5-VL -- the strongest
published baseline we are aware of for Qwen. DASH \cite{dash}
clusters \emph{images} that systematically trigger hallucinations.
Visual CounterFact \cite{pixelsvspriors} studies the
prior-vs-pixels conflict when an image contradicts world knowledge.

\paragraph{Our differentiator.} Unlike the works above, PhantomMap
operates entirely through the VLM's prompted bounding-box output --
the same channel exposed to an end user -- and is the first to
profile the spatial distribution of hallucinated objects in pixel
space. The geometry of the phantom box itself, paired with the token
logprobs the model emits alongside it, supplies a detection signal
strong enough to outperform HALP's internal-feature detector on both
models we tested.

%%%%%%%%% METHOD
\section{Method}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig2_method.pdf}
\caption{PhantomMap pipeline. For each $(I, o)$ pair in POPE, the
VLM is prompted for a yes/no answer and, if yes, the bounding box of
$o$. Where ground truth is ``no'', every ``yes'' is a hallucination
event and the bbox is its \emph{phantom}. Phantom geometry plus
coordinate-token logprobs feed the atlas (\S\ref{sec:atlas}) and the
detector (\S\ref{sec:detector}).}
\label{fig:method}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Elicitation prompt.} For every (image $I$, query object $o$)
pair we issue a single prompt:
\begin{quote}\small
\texttt{Look at the image. Is there a \{o\} in the image? Answer
``yes'' or ``no''. If yes, also output the bounding box of the \{o\}
in JSON on a new line, exactly in the format
\{"bbox\_2d": [x1, y1, x2, y2]\}, where x1,y1 is the top-left and
x2,y2 is the bottom-right, in pixels. If no, output nothing else.}
\end{quote}
The prompt is fixed and identical across models. No few-shot
examples, no chain-of-thought prefix. Outputs are parsed tolerantly
(handling markdown fences, list-of-dicts variants, and LLaVA's
normalised $[0,1]$ coordinate convention; \S\ref{sec:exp}) and
validated to lie within $[0, W] \times [0, H]$ with a minimum side of
$4$ px. For every generated token we capture its greedy
log-probability.

\paragraph{Phantom event.} A \emph{phantom event} is a triple
$(I, o, b)$ where POPE labels $o$ as absent in $I$, the VLM answers
``yes'', and emits a valid bbox $b$. An \emph{honest event} is the
analogous triple with POPE label ``yes''; these form the natural
distribution against which phantom geometry is compared.

\paragraph{Spatial atlas.} \label{sec:atlas}
Each bbox is normalised to $[0,1]^2$ by dividing $x$ by $W$ and $y$
by $H$. We fit a 2D Gaussian kernel density estimate (bandwidth
$0.15$, via scipy \texttt{gaussian\_kde}) over the normalised centres
of each event set. The summary statistic is the \emph{centre bias}
$\bar{d} = \tfrac{1}{N}\sum_i \sqrt{(c_{x,i}-0.5)^2 + (c_{y,i}-0.5)^2}$,
with the population mean area $\bar{A} = \tfrac{1}{N}\sum_i w_i h_i$
reported alongside.

\paragraph{Training-free detector.} \label{sec:detector}
For every ``yes'' event with a valid bbox we compute eight features:
the normalised centre $(c_x, c_y)$, width $w$ and height $h$, area
$wh$, centre distance $d_\text{center}$, aspect ratio $(x_2 -
x_1)/(y_2 - y_1)$, the mean and min log-probability of the four
coordinate tokens, and the log-probability of the yes/no token (or
the first content-carrying token when yes/no is omitted). Labels are
$1$ for phantoms, $0$ for honest. Features are standardised; we fit
an $\ell_2$-regularised logistic regression ($C=1$) and report AUROC
on a stratified $70/30$ split. Baselines: chance
($\mathrm{AUROC}=0.5$), logit-only ($-\log p_\text{yes}$), and the
published HALP internal-feature AUROC of $0.787$ \cite{halp}.

\section{Experiments and Results} \label{sec:exp}

\paragraph{Models and data.} Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct \cite{qwen25vl}
and LLaVA-NeXT-Mistral-7B \cite{llavanext}, both at bf16 on a single
NVIDIA L4 GPU via Hugging Face \texttt{transformers}~4.57 with
greedy decoding. Inputs: POPE random / popular / adversarial, each
$3{,}000$ (image, object) probes over COCO val2014 \cite{mscoco},
$9{,}000$ probes per model. A single complete sweep for both models
completes in $\sim\!6$ hours.

\paragraph{Model-specific output formats.} Qwen2.5-VL emits pixel
coordinates in a single $\{$\texttt{bbox\_2d}:[...]$\}$ dict or a
list of such dicts; it also frequently skips the required yes/no
preamble and jumps straight to the bbox (we parse this as implicit
``yes''). LLaVA-NeXT emits normalised $[0,1]$ coordinates and
answers ``yes'' without a bounding box on $81\%$ of its positive
responses. Our parser handles all three quirks; no other
model-specific code is needed.

%%%%%%%%% ANALYSIS
\section{Analysis and Discussion}

\paragraph{Why do phantoms live at the edge?} Two explanations are
consistent with the data. First, at adversarial negatives the model
is asked about objects that \emph{co-occur} with visible ones in
COCO; the model's grounding head has to place the phantom
\emph{somewhere}, and small peripheral regions attract lower
attention and therefore less scrutiny during training. Second, our
Qwen phantom area is $37\%$ smaller than the honest area, which is
consistent with the hypothesis that models hedge unseen objects with
smaller boxes, reducing the cost of being wrong. A controlled
manipulation of the grounding supervision -- e.g. by fine-tuning
Qwen with only centre-cropped objects -- would test this directly.

\paragraph{Why does geometry only help Qwen?} Qwen grounds on
virtually every ``yes'' answer ($100\%$ bbox coverage), giving the
detector a clean shape signal at every instance. LLaVA only grounds
on $19\%$ of its positives, and the boxes it does emit are
quantised to normalised coordinates -- so its bbox geometry carries
less information per event than Qwen's, and the yes-token logprob
alone already captures most of the separating signal.

\paragraph{Where the detector fails.} False negatives
(missed phantoms) cluster around high-frequency COCO categories
(``person'', ``chair'', ``dining table''); the model emits
plausibly-sized, plausibly-centred boxes for these, hiding them
among honest examples. False positives are dominated by honest
``yes'' answers with small, off-centre objects (truncations, crops).
This suggests that the atlas structure we exploit is meaningful but
not a single knob -- the detector is learning a multi-dimensional
boundary.

%%%%%%%%% CONCLUSION
\section{Conclusion}

PhantomMap treats a VLM's hallucinations the way a cartographer
treats uncharted territory: we map them. Using only the VLM's public
bbox-output interface on POPE, we show that phantom objects are
placed with a predictable spatial signature -- smaller, further from
centre -- across two 7B-scale VLMs trained with very different
grounding recipes. This signature, combined with coordinate-token
confidence, feeds a training-free logistic regression detector that
reaches $\mathrm{AUROC}=0.916$ on Qwen2.5-VL and $0.873$ on
LLaVA-NeXT, both exceeding the strongest previously-published
internal-feature baseline. Where a hallucination is drawn turns out
to carry at least as much information as what it names, and it is
accessible to anyone with API access to the model.

%%%%%%%%% ETHICS
\section{Ethical and Societal Considerations}
Hallucination detection is a safety signal but also a red-team
vector: the same features that identify hallucinations could help
adversaries craft queries that hide them. We release code under an
academic-use licence and add no new images to the public domain.
POPE, derived from COCO, carries the same consent and bias
properties as its source; our atlas therefore inherits those biases.
We flag model-specific signatures so downstream users can account
for them.
